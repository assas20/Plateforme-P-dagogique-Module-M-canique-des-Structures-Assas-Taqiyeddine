    1. Influence Lines of determinate Structures

    I.1. Defintion

    An influence line represents the variation of the reaction, shear, moment, or deflection at a specific point in a member as a concentrated force moves over the member. Once this line is constructed, one can tell at a glance where the moving load should be placed on the structure so that it creates the greatest influence at the specified point. Furthermore, the magnitude of the associated reaction, shear, moment, or deflection at the point can then be calculated from the ordinates of the influence line diagram. For these reasons, influence lines play an important part in the design of bridges, industrial crane rails, conveyors, and other structures where loads move across their span.

    I.2 Influence Lines for Beams

    I.2.1Procedure for Analysis(Analytical Method)

    Either of the following two procedures can be used to construct the influence line at a specific point P in a member for any function (reaction, shear, or moment). For both of these procedures we will choose the moving force to have a dimensionless magnitude of unity.

    I.2.1 Tabulate Values

    • Place a unit load \(P = 1\)at different positions \(x\)along the member.

    • For each position, use statics to calculate the required quantity: reaction \(R\), shear force \(V\), or bending moment \(M\).

    • Apply the adopted sign conventions.

    • For a statically determinate beam, the influence line consists of straight-line segments.

    • Prepare a table showing the unit-load position \(x\)and the corresponding value of \(R\), \(V\), or \(M\).

    • Plot the calculated values and connect the points to construct the influence line.

    I.2.2 Influence-Line Equations

    Rather than placing the unit load at specific points on the beam and plotting the values of Ay for each point, we can place the unit load a variable distance x from A, Fig 6. The reaction Ay as a function of x can be determined from.

    Influence Line Equation

    The equation expressing the reaction \(A_{y}\) as a function of the position \(x\) of the unit load (\(P = 1\)) on the \(10\text{~m}\) beam is:

    \[A_{y}(x) = 1 - \frac{1}{10}x\quad\text{for~}0 \leq x \leq 10\text{~m}\]

    Step-by-Step Derivation Method

    1. Definition of distances :

      • The total span of the beam is \(L = 10\text{~m}\).

      • The unit load \(P = 1\) is located at a distance \(x\) from support \(A\).

      • The lever arm of the load \(P\) relative to support \(B\) is \((10 - x)\).

    2. Moment equilibrium equation about \(B\) (\(\sum_{}^{}M_{B} = 0\)):

    Taking counterclockwise as positive, the reaction \(A_{y}\) creates a clockwise (negative) moment and the unit load \(1\) creates a counterclockwise (positive) moment about \(B\):

    \[- A_{y} \cdot (10) + 1 \cdot (10 - x) = 0\]

    1. Isolating \(A_{y}\):

    \[10 \cdot A_{y} = 10 - x\]

    \[A_{y} = \frac{10 - x}{10} = 1 - \frac{1}{10}x\]

    Key Point Verification

    • \(x = 0\text{~m}\): \(A_{y} = 1 - \frac{0}{10} = 1\)

    • \(x = 2.5\text{~m}\): \(A_{y} = 1 - \frac{2.5}{10} = 0.75\)

    • \(x = 5\text{~m}\): \(A_{y} = 1 - \frac{5}{10} = 0.5\)

    • \(x = 7.5\text{~m}\): \(A_{y} = 1 - \frac{7.5}{10} = 0.25\)

    • \(x = 10\text{~m}\): \(A_{y} = 1 - \frac{10}{10} = 0\)

    I.3 MÜLLER-BRESLAU PRINCIPLE : QUALITATIVE KINEMATIC APPROACH

    In 1886, Heinrich Müller-Breslau developed a technique for quickly determining the shape of an influence line.

    The Müller-Breslau principle is based on the principle of virtual work and provides a simple way to construct the qualitative shape of influence lines.

    Principle

    The qualitative shape of the influence line for a given function, such as:

    • Support reaction (R)

    • Shear force (T) or (V)

    • Bending moment (M)

    can be obtained by:

    1. Releasing the restraint corresponding to the function being studied at the section where the influence line is required.

    2. Applying a unit action corresponding to this function in its positive direction.

    3. Allowing the modified structure to deform.

    The deflected shape of the modified structure represents the qualitative shape of the influence line.

    Easy rule to remember

    RELEASE → APPLY → DEFORM → INFLUENCE LINE

    Example 1: Influence Line for the Vertical Reaction (Ay)

    Consider the beam shown in Fig. 23.a.

    We want to determine the qualitative shape of the influence line for the vertical reaction (Ay) at A.

    Step 1 – Release the reaction

    Replace the pin at A with a roller guide, as shown in Fig. 23.b.

    The roller guide still allows the beam to resist the horizontal force, but it cannot resist the vertical reaction (Ay).

    Step 2 – Apply the positive action

    Apply a positive upward force (Ay) at A.

    Step 3 – Observe the deformation

    The beam deflects into the dashed position shown in Fig. 23.c.

    Conclusion

    The dashed deflected shape in Fig. 23.c represents the qualitative shape of the influence line for (Ay).

    Remember:
    Release (Ay) → Apply (+Ay) → Observe the deflected shape → Influence line for (Ay).

    I.4. Influence Lines for Floor Girders

    In a floor system, loads are transferred from the slab to the floor beams, then to the girder, and finally to the columns. The girder is simply supported by the columns. As shown in Fig. 29.a, the load is transferred to the girder at specific points A, B, C, and D, called panel points. The part between two panel points, such as B–C in Fig. 29.b, is called a panel. These points are important for drawing the influence lines of the girder.

    The influence line for a specified point on the girder can be determined using the same statics procedure, place the unit load at various points x on the floor slab and always compute the function (shear or moment) at the specified point P in the girder, Fig. 29.b. Plotting these values versus x yields the influence line for the function at P.

    For example, when the unit load is placed on the floor slab, as shown in Fig. 29.b:

    1. Determine the slab reactions \(F_{B}\ \)and \(F_{C}\).

    2. Use these reactions to calculate the girder support reactions \(F_{A\ }\)and \(F_{D}\).

    3. Cut the girder at point \(P\)and use the method of sections to determine the internal shear \(V_{P\ }\)and internal moment\({\ M}_{P\ }\).

    \[V_{P\ } = R_{A\ } - F_{B}\]

    \({\ M}_{P\ }\)​=\(\ R_{A\ }\)x−\(F_{B}\)​(x−s)

    where:

    • \(V_{P}\)= internal shear at point \(P\)

    • \(M_{P}\)= internal moment at point \(P\)

    • \(R_{A}\)= girder reaction at \(A\)

    • \(F_{B}\)= reaction from the floor slab at \(B\)

    • \(x\)= distance from \(A\)to \(P\)

    • \(s\)= distance from \(A\)to \(B\)

    Important : \(V_{P}\)is constant within the panel \(BC\), whereas \(M_{P}\)depends on the exact position of \(P\).

    Step-by-Step Analysis

    1. Slab Reactions (\(\mathbf{F}_{\mathbf{B}}\) and \(\mathbf{F}_{\mathbf{C}}\))

    For a unit load acting on slab panel \(BC\) at distance \(x\) from \(A\):

    • Span of the panel segment = \(s\)

    • Position of unit load relative to point \(B\) = \(x - s\)

    Applying static equilibrium to the slab span \(BC\):

    • \(\sum_{}^{}M_{C} = 0 \Longrightarrow F_{B} \cdot s = 1 \cdot (2s - x)\)

    \[F_{B} = \frac{2s - x}{s} = 2 - \frac{x}{s}\]

    • \(\sum_{}^{}M_{B} = 0 \Longrightarrow F_{C} \cdot s = 1 \cdot (x - s)\)

    \[F_{C} = \frac{x - s}{s} = \frac{x}{s} - 1\]

    Girder Support Reactions (\(\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{A}}\) and \(\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{D}}\))

    The total length of the girder is \(L = 3s\). The loads transmitted from the slab through the floor beams onto the girder are \(F_{B}\) at point \(B\) (\(x = s\)) and \(F_{C}\) at point \(C\) (\(x = 2s\)).

    Applying equilibrium to the entire girder:

    • Taking moments about \(D\) (\(x = 3s\)):

    \[\sum_{}^{}M_{D} = 0 \Longrightarrow R_{A}(3s) = F_{B}(2s) + F_{C}(s)\]

    \[R_{A}(3s) = \left( 2 - \frac{x}{s} \right)(2s) + \left( \frac{x}{s} - 1 \right)(s) = 3s - x\]

    \[R_{A} = \frac{3s - x}{3s} = 1 - \frac{x}{3s}\]

    • Taking moments about \(A\) (\(x = 0\)):

    \[\sum_{}^{}M_{A} = 0 \Longrightarrow R_{D}(3s) = F_{B}(s) + F_{C}(2s)\]

    \[R_{D}(3s) = \left( 2 - \frac{x}{s} \right)(s) + \left( \frac{x}{s} - 1 \right)(2s) = x\]

    \[R_{D} = \frac{x}{3s}\]

    1. Internal Forces at Point \(\mathbf{P}\) (\(\mathbf{V}_{\mathbf{P}}\) and \(\mathbf{M}_{\mathbf{P}}\))

    Consider a section cut at point \(P\) located at distance \(d\) from support \(A\) (where \(s < d < 2s\)). Isolate the left segment of the girder (\(A\) to \(P\)) Fig29.c and Fig 29.d:

    • Internal Shear (\(V_{P}\)):

    \[\sum_{}^{}F_{y} = 0 \Longrightarrow R_{A} - F_{B} - V_{P} = 0\]

    \[V_{P} = R_{A} - F_{B} = \left( 1 - \frac{x}{3s} \right) - \left( 2 - \frac{x}{s} \right) = - 1 + \frac{2x}{3s}\]

    (Note: \(V_{P}\) depends only on the unit load position \(x\), remaining uniform for any point \(P\) within panel \(BC\).)

    • Internal Moment (\(\mathbf{M}_{\mathbf{P}}\)):

    \[\sum_{}^{}M_{P} = 0 \Longrightarrow M_{P} = R_{A} \cdot d - F_{B} \cdot (d - s)\]

    \[M_{P} = \left( 1 - \frac{x}{3s} \right)d - \left( 2 - \frac{x}{s} \right)(d - s)\]

    \[M_{P} = 2s - d - x + \frac{2xd}{3s}\]

    Here is the calculation summary for the three unit load positions (measuring distance \(x\) from support \(A\)):

    Case 1: Unit Load at Point \(\mathbf{B}\) (\(\mathbf{x = s}\))

    • Slab Reactions:

      • \(F_{B} = 2 - \frac{s}{s} = \mathbf{1}\)

      • \(F_{C} = \frac{s}{s} - 1 = \mathbf{0}\)

    • Girder Support Reactions:

      • \(R_{A} = 1 - \frac{s}{3s} = \frac{\mathbf{2}}{\mathbf{3}}\)

      • \(R_{D} = \frac{s}{3s} = \frac{\mathbf{1}}{\mathbf{3}}\)

    • Internal Forces at Point \(P\):

      • Shear force: \(V_{P} = - 1 + \frac{2(s)}{3s} = \mathbf{-}\frac{\mathbf{1}}{\mathbf{3}}\)

      • Bending moment: \(M_{P} = \left( \frac{2}{3} \right)d - 1(d - s) = \mathbf{s -}\frac{\mathbf{1}}{\mathbf{3}}\mathbf{d}\)

    Intermediate Case: Unit Load Between \(\mathbf{B}\) and \(\mathbf{C}\) (Midpoint of Panel, \(\mathbf{x = 1.5}\mathbf{s =}\frac{\mathbf{3}}{\mathbf{2}}\mathbf{s}\))

    The midpoint of panel \(BC\) is located at distance \(x = s + 0.5s = 1.5s\) from support \(A\).

    • Slab Reactions:

      • \(F_{B} = 2 - \frac{1.5s}{s} = \frac{\mathbf{1}}{\mathbf{2}}\)

      • \(F_{C} = \frac{1.5s}{s} - 1 = \frac{\mathbf{1}}{\mathbf{2}}\)

    • Girder Support Reactions:

      • \(R_{A} = 1 - \frac{1.5s}{3s} = \frac{\mathbf{1}}{\mathbf{2}}\)

      • \(R_{D} = \frac{1.5s}{3s} = \frac{\mathbf{1}}{\mathbf{2}}\)

    • Internal Forces at Point \(P\):

      • Shear force: \(V_{P} = R_{A} - F_{B} = \frac{1}{2} - \frac{1}{2} = \mathbf{0}\)

      • Bending moment: \(M_{P} = \left( \frac{1}{2} \right)d - \frac{1}{2}(d - s) = \frac{\mathbf{1}}{\mathbf{2}}\mathbf{s}\)

    Case 2: Unit Load at Point \(\mathbf{C}\) (\(\mathbf{x = 2}\mathbf{s}\))

    • Slab Reactions:

      • \(F_{B} = 2 - \frac{2s}{s} = \mathbf{0}\)

      • \(F_{C} = \frac{2s}{s} - 1 = \mathbf{1}\)

    • Girder Support Reactions:

      • \(R_{A} = 1 - \frac{2s}{3s} = \frac{\mathbf{1}}{\mathbf{3}}\)

      • \(R_{D} = \frac{2s}{3s} = \frac{\mathbf{2}}{\mathbf{3}}\)

    • Internal Forces at Point \(P\):

      • Shear force: \(V_{P} = - 1 + \frac{2(2s)}{3s} = \frac{\mathbf{1}}{\mathbf{3}}\)

      • Bending moment: \(M_{P} = \left( \frac{1}{3} \right)d - 0(d - s) = \frac{\mathbf{1}}{\mathbf{3}}\mathbf{d}\)

    What this means for the Influence Line (IL)Because the transmitted panel loads \(F_{B}\ \)and \(F_{c\ \ }\)vary linearly (as straight lines) between B and C, any structural effect in the main girder (support reactions, shear force \(V_{P}\), or bending moment \(M_{P}\)) must also vary linearly as a straight line across panel BC

    💡The Golden Rule in Practice

    • Thanks to this fundamental property, you never need to set up equations for load positions in the middle of a panel!To draw a complete Influence Line for an indirectly loaded floor system:Calculate the effect only when the unit load is placed at each panel point (A, B, C, D, etc.).Plot these specific values on your diagram.Connect adjacent points using straight line segments.

    • As long as the load remains in panel \(CD\), panel \(BC\) receives no direct transferred load. The internal shear passing through this entire panel remains completely constant from \(B\) to \(C\), equal to the support reaction \(R_{A}\).

    I.5 Influence Lines for Trusses

    Trusses are often used as primary load-carrying elements for bridges. Hence, for design it is important to be able to construct the influence lines for each of its members. As shown in Fig. 33, the loading on the bridge deck is transmitted to stringers, which in turn transmit the loading to floor beams and then to the joints along the bottom cord of the truss.

    Trusses are commonly used to support bridge loads. To draw the influence line of a truss member, a unit load is placed successively at each joint along the bottom chord. The force in the member is then calculated using the method of joints or method of sections. The calculated forces are plotted against the load positions. Tension is taken as positive (+) and compression as negative (−). Finally, the points are connected with straight lines to obtain the influence line.

    I.5 Influence Lines for frame

    For a structural frame, influence lines can be used to determine the support reactions, shear force, and bending moment at a specific point. A unit load (\(W = 1\)) is moved along the frame, and the corresponding value of the required function is calculated for each load position. These values are then plotted against the load position to obtain the influence line.

    Key Points:

    • Move a unit load (\(W = 1\)) along the frame.

    • Calculate the desired function (reaction, shear force, or bending moment) at the specified point.

    • Plot the calculated values versus the load position (\(x\)).

    • Connect the key points with straight lines.
